In finite-dimensional linear algebra, an operation with a left inverse also has a right inverse. A group algebra keeps finite sums but may lose finite dimension. Irving Kaplansky asked whether that surviving finiteness still forces an inverse to work in both orders. His characteristic-zero theorem, and Susan Montgomery’s 1969 alternative proof, left positive characteristic as the difficult extension.

A parallel question came from Walter Gottschalk in 1973: must a local, translation-symmetric rule on all group configurations be onto whenever it is one-to-one? Gromov’s finite-approximation approach and Weiss’s sofic terminology linked dynamics to finite models. Ara, O’Meara and Perera established broad algebraic positive cases; Elek and Szabó extended them to sofic groups. Their work also made the finite-field bridge between the two questions explicit. Another branch organized finite cancellation patterns into universal groups.

The 2026 family reports failures beyond these protected classes, including a torsion-free example over the field of two elements. Its companions have different coefficient and torsion hypotheses. The determinant counterexample follows a separate construction, even though finite approximation connects its history to the same landscape.
