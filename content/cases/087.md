Mahler’s volume-product question began in the geometry of numbers: a convex body and its polar helped translate between lattice problems. His [1939 transference paper](https://carmamaths.org/resources/mahler/docs/057.pdf) explicitly guessed the symmetric sharp bound, displaying a parallelotope and its dual as examples. An accompanying [polygon paper](https://carmamaths.org/resources/mahler/docs/059.pdf), dated August 1938, already proved planar inequalities—but expressly left equality for arbitrary convex regions unsettled.

The eventual equality story grew beyond those examples. [Hanner’s 1956 paper](https://journals.msp.org/mscand/article/view/2863) concerned intersections of translates; its recursively constructed bodies later supplied a much richer family of candidate minimizers. Functional versions subsequently replaced shapes by log-concave functions and polarity by convex duality.

An especially surprising turn came when [Artstein-Avidan, Karasev and Ostrover](https://arxiv.org/html/1303.4197v2) connected the symmetric conjecture to symplectic geometry and billiards. Their capacity calculation did not itself produce the ball embeddings now claimed.

The 2026 family joins several endpoints: symmetric Hanner extremizers, nonsymmetric simplex extremizers, functional lower inequalities and symplectic width. Calling all of these “Mahler’s conjecture” compresses decades of changing objects and formulations. That interpretation should preserve the separate claims and their separate equality questions.
